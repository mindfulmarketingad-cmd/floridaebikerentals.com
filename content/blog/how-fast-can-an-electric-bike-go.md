It's the first thing almost everyone asks when they swing a leg over an e-bike: how fast does this
thing go? The honest answer is "faster than you'd expect off the line, and slower than you'd guess
at the top." A legal electric bicycle in Florida stops helping you at 20 mph, or 28 mph for a Class 3
bike. Past that point the motor goes quiet and it's your legs, the wind and the road doing the work.

We spend our days sorting through e-bike rental shops, reading what they rent and answering riders'
questions, and speed comes up more than anything except price. So here's how the speed of an e-bike
actually works, what changes it on the day, and how fast you'll really be riding on a Florida path.

## The short answer: 20 mph, or 28 mph for Class 3

Florida sorts electric bicycles into three classes, the same way most US states do. The definitions
are in section 316.003 of the Florida Statutes, and all three share one rule: the motor can't be more
than 750 watts.

- **Class 1:** the motor helps only while you pedal, and stops helping at **20 mph**.
- **Class 2:** the motor can also run on a throttle, with no pedalling, and still stops at **20 mph**.
- **Class 3:** the motor helps only while you pedal, and stops helping at **28 mph**.

Federal product law draws a similar line. A "low-speed electric bicycle" under 15 U.S.C. 2085 has
working pedals, a motor under 750 watts, and a top speed under 20 mph on motor power alone. That's
why so many bikes sold in the US are tuned to 20 mph out of the box.

Our [e-bike classes guide](/blog/ebike-classes-explained/) goes deeper on throttles and pedal assist,
and our [Florida e-bike law guide](/blog/florida-ebike-laws/) covers ages, helmets and where you can
ride.

## "Stops helping" isn't the same as "can't go faster"

This is the part that surprises people. The 20 or 28 mph figure isn't a speed governor on the bike.
It's the point where the motor cuts its assistance. Nothing stops you pedalling harder or rolling
down a bridge faster than that; the motor simply stops adding power once the speed sensor reads the
limit.

In practice, a 60 to 70 lb e-bike with upright handlebars doesn't want to go much past its assist
limit on flat ground. The moment the motor drops out, you feel the full weight of the bike and the
wind. Most riders settle back just under the cut-off without thinking about it.

## What actually sets your speed on a ride

The class tells you the ceiling. These are the things that decide where you sit under it.

### The motor and the controller

E-bike motors come in two main layouts. A **hub motor** sits in the middle of the front or rear wheel
and pushes the wheel directly. A **mid-drive motor** sits between the pedals and drives the chain, so
it works through the bike's gears. Hub motors are simpler and common on rentals; mid-drives tend to
feel more natural on hills and hold speed better on a long climb.

Between the battery and the motor sits the **controller**, the small computer that decides how much
power to send. It reads your pedalling, your assist level and your speed, and it's the controller
that tapers the motor off as you approach 20 or 28 mph.

### Pedal-assist level

Most e-bikes have three to five assist levels. On a low level the motor adds a little help and you
might cruise at 10 to 13 mph. On the top level it adds a lot, and you'll reach the assist limit with
easy pedalling. Speed is mostly a choice you make with the button on the handlebar.

### Cadence sensor or torque sensor

How the bike decides to help matters as much as how strong the motor is.

- A **cadence sensor** notices that the pedals are turning and switches the motor on. It feels a bit
  like cruise control: pedal lightly and the bike still surges forward.
- A **torque sensor** measures how hard you're pushing and matches it. It feels more like a normal
  bike with stronger legs, and it's smoother in crowds because the bike only goes as hard as you do.

Many rental fleets use cadence sensors because they're simple and forgiving for first-time riders.

### Battery charge

On many hub-motor bikes, top speed drops a little as the battery runs down, because the voltage sags
under load. A bike that hits 20 mph easily on a full charge may struggle to hold it at the end of a
long day. Heat can do the same thing: battery and motor electronics protect themselves by reducing
power when they get hot, which matters on a July afternoon in Florida.

### Tires, weight and wind

- **Fat tires** grip sand and shell beautifully, but those wide, low-pressure tires roll slower on
  pavement. Our [fat-tire e-bike guide](/electric-bike-types/fat-tire-electric-bikes/) explains the trade.
- **Tire pressure** matters more than people think. A soft tire feels sluggish and drains the battery.
- **Rider and cargo weight** slow acceleration and climbing, though on flat ground the motor hides most of it.
- **Wind** is the big one on the coast. A steady headwind on a beach road can knock several mph off
  your cruising speed, even with the motor working hard.

## Why going faster costs so much more battery

Air resistance grows with the square of your speed, and the power needed to push through it grows
roughly with the cube. Riding at 20 mph instead of 15 mph takes about 2.4 times the power just to
beat the air (20 ÷ 15, cubed). That's why a battery that lasts all day at a relaxed pace can run
short when you ride flat-out on the top assist level.

If you're renting for a long day, the easiest way to stretch your range is to drop one assist level
and cruise a few mph slower. You'll barely feel the difference in time, and you'll feel a big
difference in battery.

## How fast you'll really ride on a Florida path

On a shared path with walkers, dogs, kids on scooters and other cyclists, the bike's top speed
rarely matters. Most of the time you'll ride well under the assist limit, simply because that's what
the path allows. Some towns and trails post their own lower limits or ban e-bikes from certain paths
and sidewalks, so read the signs where you ride and ask your rental shop what applies locally.

A few numbers worth keeping in your head:

- At **20 mph** you cover about **29 feet every second**. At 15 mph it's 22 feet.
- Stopping distance grows with the square of your speed, so going a third faster means needing
  roughly 1.8 times the room to stop.
- E-bikes are heavier than regular bikes, so they take longer to stop from the same speed.

Our view: ride a busy beach path at a pace where you could stop for a kid stepping out from behind a
parked car, and save the top assist level for open stretches.
## What about e-bikes that go 30 mph or more?

You'll see electric bikes online advertised at 30, 40 or even 50 mph. Those either have motors over
750 watts, assist past 28 mph, or have been "unlocked" or modified. They don't meet Florida's
definition of an electric bicycle, so they're not treated as bicycles under state law. Rental shops
in Florida rent legal Class 1, 2 or 3 e-bikes, and modifying a rental to go faster will almost
certainly break your rental agreement.

## Which class should you rent?

- **First ride, beach town, family day:** a Class 1 or Class 2 bike. The throttle on a Class 2 is
  handy for starting from a stop on sand or at a crossing.
- **Longer, open rides:** Class 3 can make sense on long, quiet roads, but on most Florida trails and
  beach paths you won't use the extra speed, and some paths don't allow Class 3 at all.
- **Not sure?** Tell the shop where you plan to ride and let them suggest the bike.

## FAQs

### How fast can an electric bicycle go in Florida?

A legal electric bicycle in Florida gives motor assistance up to 20 mph for Class 1 and Class 2
bikes, and up to 28 mph for Class 3 bikes. You can pedal or coast faster than that, but the motor
stops helping at the limit. The motor can't be more than 750 watts.

### Can an e-bike go faster than 20 mph?

Yes, under your own power or downhill. A Class 1 or Class 2 e-bike's motor stops assisting at
20 mph, but nothing stops you pedalling faster. In practice the bike's weight makes it hard to hold
much more than that on flat ground.

### How fast does a throttle e-bike go?

A Class 2 e-bike's throttle can move the bike without pedalling, up to 20 mph. Class 1 and Class 3
bikes don't have a throttle that works above walking speed.

### Why is my e-bike slower than it should be?

The usual causes are a low battery, a low assist level, soft tires, a headwind, heat, or a heavy
load. Fat tires are also naturally slower on pavement. If a rental feels weak on a full charge, take
it back to the shop and ask them to check it.

### Do I need a licence to ride a fast e-bike in Florida?

No licence, registration or insurance is needed for a legal Class 1, 2 or 3 e-bike in Florida. Riders
and passengers under 16 must wear a helmet. Bikes that go beyond the legal e-bike limits aren't
treated as bicycles, and different rules apply.
